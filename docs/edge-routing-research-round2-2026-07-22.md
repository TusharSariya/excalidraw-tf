=== 1. METRIC samePairMultiCross === Owner pattern (2): "edge pairs that cross multiple times".

Definition: over `pairRecords`, a pair OFFENDS when deduped `events.length ≥ 2`.

=== 3. METRIC backwardTurn === Owner pattern (4): "edges that turn backwards against LR flow".

Per EdgeGeom, over polyline points p_0..p_n (world coords, LR flow = +x):

1. Per-segment dx*i = x*{i+1} − x_i. Compress consecutive segments into maximal signed runs by sign(dx) (|dx| < 1e-6 is neutral and folds into the current run with 0 contribution).
2. Discard (fold into surrounding context) any run with |Δx_run| ≤ H_BACKTRACK = 20 px, then re-merge adjacent same-sign runs and repeat until stable. Justification for H = 20: STRATA_EDGE_STYLE_STUB_PX = STRATA_CHANNEL_STUB_PX = 20 — a perpendicular escape/entry stub can legitimately move ≤20px against flow (e.g. entering a target's right side), and curve-sampling jitter (14-pt bezier) is far below 20px per run; anything beyond one stub length is a genuine backward excursion.

# refinement-algorithms

## Summary

Post-layout (fixed-node) fixes exist for all five owner-flagged defect classes, and all compose with the existing 14-pt curve sampler + never-worse guard. Cheapest/highest-confidence first: (A) endpoint-occlusion clamp + port fan-out (pure local geometry, closed-form-ish, near-zero risk), (B) x-monotone control-point clamp killing backward turns AND same-pair multi-crossings simultaneously for forward edges (monotone curves cross each other at most a bounded number of times; two x-monotone curves that are function graphs cross like polylines), (C) same-pair multi-crossing "lens removal" (Reidemeister-II-style bigon reduction — detect crossing pairs of the same 2 edges that are adjacent along both curves, swap the sub-paths between the two crossing points; strictly removes 2 crossings, guard-compatible), (D) crossing-angle steering via the Demel et al. GD'18 greedy/random-sampling heuristic adapted to bezier control points (realistic gain: worst-angle up from ~10-20° toward 40-60°, not to 90°; local, few-hundred-ms feasible at 145 routable edges), (E) parallel-cross organization via shared-locus alignment (poor man's block crossing) — full block-crossing minimization is NP-hard and overkill at this scale. Recommended build order: 2 → 3 → 1 → 4 → 5.

## Per-defect algorithm findings (fixed node positions, ~322 edges / 145 routable, budget few hundred ms)

### 1. SAME-PAIR MULTI-CROSSINGS — lens (bigon) removal, Reidemeister-II style

Mechanics: For each edge pair (e,f) with ≥2 intersections between their polylines, take two crossings p,q that are consecutive along BOTH curves (a "lens" / empty bigon in the arrangement literature — see the star-simple-drawing lens machinery, jgaa-00600). Swap the sub-polylines of e and f between p and q (equivalently: reroute e through f's sub-path and vice versa, then smooth). This removes exactly those 2 crossings, changes nothing outside the lens, and cannot create new crossings with third edges if you keep the union of the two sub-paths (the swapped geometry occupies the same point set). This is the standard "removing even/adjacent crossings" redrawing move (cf. "Adjacent Crossings Do Matter", jgaa-00266; "Simplifying Non-Simple Fan-Planar Drawings", arXiv:2108.13345 — same redrawing-procedure idea). After the swap, re-fit/re-sample the bezier through the swapped waypoints, then run the never-worse guard; if smoothing re-introduces a crossing, keep the raw swapped polyline for that edge. Detection cost: brute force segment intersection over sampled polylines = 145² /2 pairs × 14×14 segments ≈ 2M segment tests ≈ 10-30 ms in JS; a sweep or per-rank-corridor bucketing makes it trivial. Iterate to fixpoint (each pass strictly decreases crossing count, so it terminates; in practice 1-2 passes). Important structural note (confirms the task's premise): two x-monotone curves that are graphs of functions over x cross only where their vertical order flips — same as straight segments per shared x-interval. So defect class 1 is almost entirely _caused_ by non-monotone curve geometry, and fix #3 (monotone clamp) prevents most of it for forward edges; lens removal is the mop-up for residual cases (flat edges, back-edges, channel-routed orthogonals). Composability: pure post-pass on sampled polylines; guard-compatible (strict crossing decrease). Works on step/channel output too.

### 2. ENDPOINT OCCLUSION / OWN-NODE RE-ENTRY — clamp + port fan-out

(a) Own-node re-entry clamp: exact cubic-bezier-vs-axis-aligned-rect intersection is a degree-3 root solve per rect side (Bezier clipping), but the sampled 14-pt polyline check is already sufficient at your sampling density: test segments 2..13 against the source/target rect inflated by a margin m (segment 1 legitimately touches the rect). On hit, shorten the offending control arm: your curve is P0,P1,P2,P3 with perpendicular exit stubs, so P1 = P0 + d·n̂ (n̂ = outward normal). Halve d (or binary-search d ∈ [minStub, d0], ~5 iterations) until the sampled curve clears the rect. This is exactly the fix dot needed historically — Gansner et al. TSE93 notes early spline heuristics "bend sharply to turn inside virtual node boxes"; dot's answer was region-constrained splines (piecewise fit inside a polygonal corridor that excludes node boxes — Dobkin/Gansner/Koutsofios/North, "Implementing a General-Purpose Edge Router", GD'97: shortest path in corridor, then bezier fit with containment check, subdivide on failure). Your cheap analog: clamp-and-recheck instead of full corridor fitting. Cost: O(edges × 12 segs × ≤4 sides × ~5 iters) ≈ sub-ms. (b) Crossing-free endpoint neighborhood (radius r around anchors): treat each anchor's disk of radius r (≈ stub length) as a soft obstacle for OTHER edges: any foreign sampled segment entering the disk gets pushed out radially (offset the nearest waypoint to the disk boundary) — only if the guard passes. Cheap because you index waypoints in a grid. (c) Port spreading / fan-out: instead of all edges anchoring at one point per side, distribute anchors along the node side ordered by the far-endpoint's Y (the standard layered port-assignment trick; cf. "Size- and Port-Aware Horizontal Node Coordinate Assignment", GD 2015, Springer 978-3-319-27261-0*12, and ELK's port-spacing options). Sorting by opposite-endpoint angle removes crossings \_at the endpoint region* entirely between edges sharing a node (this is where the owner's "crossing right at the endpoint" complaint concentrates). O(deg log deg) per node. Directly improves your endpointRes° metric — note curve currently sits at 51.5° endpoint crowding vs baseline 23.3. Composability: (c) changes anchors, so run BEFORE curve sampling; (a)/(b) are post-passes. All guard-compatible.

### 3. BACKWARD TURNS — x-monotone bezier construction + explicit back-edge treatment

(a) Forward edges (source.x < target.x): a cubic bezier x(t) is monotone iff x'(t) ≥ 0 on [0,1]; x'(t) is a quadratic Bernstein with coefficients 3(x1−x0), 3(x2−x1), 3(x3−x2). Sufficient (and convex-hull-tight enough in practice) condition: x0 ≤ x1 ≤ x2 ≤ x3 — i.e. clamp interior control points into [x0, x3] and keep them ordered. This is the bezier analog of Fritsch–Carlson monotone cubic interpolation (there, tangent magnitudes are limited so the Hermite segment stays monotone; here, clamp x1 := max(x0, min(x1, x3)), x2 := max(x1, min(x2, x3))). With your perpendicular exit stubs on left/right node sides this is automatic; backward turns currently arise when stubs exit from top/bottom sides or when the channel router emits reversed jogs — clamp after stub construction. Zero-cost (per-edge O(1)). X-monotonicity also (i) guarantees ≤ small-bounded crossings per pair (kills defect 1 for forward-forward pairs) and (ii) removes nearFlat S-wiggles. (b) Genuine back-edges (source RIGHT of target): what dot and ELK do — both _reverse_ the edge during layering (cycle breaking: greedy heuristic in ELK, DFS-based in dot), route it through the rank structure as a normal forward edge with dummy/virtual nodes, then restore direction only for the arrowhead (TSE93 §edge splines; ELK layered reference). So at spline time there ARE no backward-turning curves in dot/ELK — the back-edge is a forward-routed spline traversed in reverse. Your fixed-position analog: route back-edges through an explicit channel _around_ the content — either (i) orbit routing: arc over the top (or under the bottom) of the spanned ranks at a Y outside the occupied band, entering/leaving vertically (this is the visually accepted "feedback arc" idiom), or (ii) reuse your strataChannelRoute corridors but reserve one dedicated top/bottom track per rank-span for reversed edges (interval-coloring already gives you tracks; tag reversed edges to the extremal track). Given LR strata with hulls, top/bottom orbit channels are the safer bet. Cost: O(#back-edges × corridor lookup), trivial — back-edges are few. Composability: (a) is a control-point filter inside the existing sampler; (b) is a routing-class switch upstream of sampling; both guard-compatible.

### 4. CROSSING-ANGLE STEERING — greedy control-point perturbation (Demel et al. GD'18 blueprint)

The directly-on-point method: Demel, Dürrschnabel, Mchedlidze, Radermacher, Wulf, "A Greedy Heuristic for Crossing-Angle Maximization" (GD 2018, LNCS 11282, 978-3-030-04414-5*20 — 1st place, GD Contest 2017): repeatedly pick the crossing with the worst angle, sample K random candidate positions for one incident endpoint (vertex), keep the sample that most improves the locally-worst crossing angle. Adaptation to fixed nodes: perturb \_bezier control points / interior waypoints* instead of vertices — pick the worst-angle crossing on the sampled polylines, identify which curve's control point most influences the local tangent at the crossing (the nearer of P1/P2 by parameter t), sample K≈20 positions in a shrinking disk, re-sample only the two affected curves, accept under (worst-local-angle improves) ∧ (never-worse guard: no new crossings/pierces). Related: Bekos et al., "A Heuristic Approach Towards Drawings of Graphs With High Crossing Resolution" (arXiv:1808.10519) — force-based angle springs (their ORTHFD/POLYFD line uses α=30° after Ware et al.'s finding that <30° crossings hurt reading); and Finkel–Tamassia "Curvilinear Graph Drawing Using the Force-Directed Method" (GD 2004) — treat control points as force-carrying dummy vertices, the canonical way to run any force pass over beziers. (SGD)² (arXiv:2112.01571) confirms crossing-angle is optimizable by local gradient moves but is overkill here. Cost: per accepted move you re-test 1-2 edges against neighbors via the grid index (~140×14 segs) — μs-scale; a budget of ~2000 candidate evaluations lands well under 200 ms at 145 edges. Realistic gain estimate: contest experience and both heuristic papers show worst-crossing-angle typically improves from near-0-20° to 40-60° on non-planar instances; pushing everything to ~90° is unattainable without moving nodes (RAC drawings need full vertex freedom and are ∃ℝ-complete to even decide — jgaa-00646). Perceptual literature (Ware et al., cited in 978-3-642-18469-7_15) says the payoff is thresholded: getting crossings above ~30° captures most of the legibility win — a realistic, valuable target; chasing 90° is not. Composability: last pass in the chain (it perturbs final geometry); guard is literally the paper's accept criterion. Note tension: steering toward 90° fights x-monotonicity if unconstrained — clamp perturbed control x-coords per fix 3(a) inside the sampler so both invariants hold.

### 5. PARALLEL-CROSS ORGANIZATION — block crossings: theory says punt, use the locus proxy

Theory: bundled/block crossing minimization is NP-hard and even APX-hard in general ("The Bundled Crossing Number", Alam et al., GD 2016, 978-3-319-50106-2_31 + arXiv:1608.08161; "Bundled Crossings Revisited", arXiv:1812.04263 — FPT/approx only in restricted settings; "Block Crossings in Storyline Visualizations", jgaa-00443 — same story for storylines). Exact block-crossing optimization at 322 edges is not a few-hundred-ms job and not needed. Practical proxy A — consistent crossing locus: for each pair of "bundles" (group edges whose sampled polylines are pairwise near-parallel and within a corridor width — cluster by (source-rank, target-rank, mean-Y band), the same grouping "Clustered Edge Routing" (Pupyrev et al. style / PacificVis 2015) and "Improving Layered Graph Layouts with Edge Bundling" (Pupyrev–Nachmanson–Kaufmann, GD 2010, 978-3-642-18469-7_30) use in layered drawings): choose one crossing x-locus x* (midpoint of the shared corridor), and locally re-time each curve so all pairwise crossings between the two bundles occur within a thin vertical slab around x* — implement by inserting a shared waypoint column: each edge of bundle A gets a waypoint at (x*, y_i) with the y-order *swapped\* to the post-crossing order, edges of bundle B likewise. The bundle-vs-bundle crossings then form one clean X-shaped block instead of a smeared braid. This is exactly the visual outcome block-crossing papers argue is more readable than the same number of scattered crossings (jgaa-00443's premise: "number of crossings alone" is the wrong metric; grouped crossings read better). Practical proxy B — within-bundle order preservation: never let edges of the same bundle cross each other (sort tracks by destination Y and keep that order end-to-end — your interval-coloring channel router can enforce this by assigning tracks in destination order). Kills owner-pattern (3) at its source: near-parallel same-bundle edges crossing each other is pure noise. Cost: clustering O(E log E); waypoint insertion O(bundle pairs). Well under budget. Composability: proxy B belongs inside strataChannelRoute's track assignment; proxy A is a waypoint pre-pass before curve sampling; both guard-checkable (count crossings before/after — proxy A keeps pair-count identical, only relocates them, so the guard must compare on the block-organization metric, not raw count — extend the guard with a "crossing-spread" term or exempt count-neutral relocations).

# metric-design

## Summary

Literature validation of the owner's 5 aesthetic claims for the strata curve-style refinement. Verdicts: (1) crossing angles → CONFIRMED with a quantitative ~70° saturation threshold; (2) same-pair multiple crossings → CONFIRMED (by theory/well-formedness; direct empirical tests thin); (3) parallel/bundled crossings → NUANCED — near-parallel crossings are bad (they ARE small-angle crossings), but ORGANIZED block crossings are theorized to be BETTER than scattered ones, so don't penalize clean bundles; (4) backward turns → CONFIRMED via geodesic-path tendency and flow-convention studies (mechanism strong, direct manipulation studies thin); (5) endpoint/node-region crossings → CONFIRMED — edge-node overlap is a validated demerit and endpoint angular resolution drives node-task errors. Biggest un-named factors the literature ranks at or above several named ones: raw crossing count ON THE READ PATH (Purchase 1997: crossings = single greatest aesthetic effect; angle does not fully absolve count on long paths), path continuity/branch count (Ware et al. 2002), and edge length. Caveat for the chosen curve style: Xu/Rooney/Passmore 2012 found strongly curved edges perform WORSE than straight — keep curvature low and endpoint tangents truthful. Owner's curve probe (92 crossings, 51.5° endpointRes) is directionally right but its high endpoint deviation is its weakest number per Ware's continuity findings.

## Findings

CLAIM 1 — "Crossing angles closer to perpendicular are more legible": CONFIRMED, with a threshold.

- Supporting: Huang, Hong, Eades, "Effects of Crossing Angles" (PacificVis 2008; RAG doc_id doi-10-1109-pacificvis-2008-4475457) and Huang et al., "Larger crossing angles make graphs easier to read" (JVLC 2014): task response time decreases roughly linearly as crossing angle grows, and the effect LEVELS OFF as the angle approaches 90°; the widely cited actionable threshold is ~70° — beyond ~70° additional gains are marginal. This is the empirical basis of the whole RAC/LAC literature (Didimo/Eades/Liotta RAC; jgaa-2700; RAC-drawability paper jgaa-2273 explicitly summarizes Huang/Eades/Hong: "large angles improve readability, but the angles do not have to be right angles").
- Mechanism (read directly from Huang's eye-tracking paper, arXiv:0810.4431, pages 3-7): near-90° crossings only slightly slow smooth eye movements; small-angle crossings cause confusion and "quite a lot of back-and-forth eye movements around the crossing points". Subject quote: "if the angle is small, you have to be careful when following the edge to make sure you end up at the right node". Path-task error rates: 0% on the no-crossing and 90°-crossing drawings vs 25% node-task errors on the acute-angle drawing.
- Nuance/refutation: same paper's discussion — "when a graph is larger and the searching path is longer, the impact of crossings can build up and become significant, EVEN IF edges cross at nearly 90 degrees." Angle maximization is not a license to add crossings. Also Huang & Huang 2010 (doi-10-1145-1865841-1865854) shows crossing NUMBER and crossing ANGLE are separate effects that interact; optimizing angle alone does not neutralize count. Kobourov et al. "Are Crossings Important for Drawing Large Graphs?" adds that at large scale, per-crossing effects wash out — what matters is crossings on the path being read.
- Design implication: treat sharp<70° as the penalty (our current sharp<70° metric is exactly the literature threshold — good); target ≥70°, not 90°. Curve probe's 0.66 sharp ratio is the number to attack; but never trade a sharp-angle fix for extra crossings on the same source-target corridor.

CLAIM 2 — "Edge pairs that cross multiple times look bad": CONFIRMED (theory + well-formedness; direct empirical tests are scarce).

- Any drawing where a pair of edges crosses twice is not a "good drawing" in the topological-graph-theory sense: a double crossing between the same pair is always removable by swapping the sub-arcs without changing anything else, so it is pure ink/confusion with zero information content. Crossing-minimal drawings never contain them. The storyline/block-crossing literature (Block Crossings in Storyline Visualizations, doi-10-7155-jgaa-00443 / arXiv:1609.00321) explicitly frames repeated pairwise crossings among the same lines as "unnecessary crossings".
- Nuance: I found no user study that isolates double-crossings specifically; the case rests on (a) they add crossings (claim-1 evidence applies twice), (b) they break monotone/good-continuation reading (Ware et al. 2002 continuity), (c) formal redundancy. Verdict CONFIRMED as a hard constraint: a post-pass that detects same-pair multi-crossings and untangles them can never hurt any measured aesthetic.

CLAIM 3 — "Parallel bundles crossing together / near-parallel edges crossing look bad": NUANCED — the two halves of this claim point in OPPOSITE directions.

- Near-parallel edges crossing each other = by definition a small crossing angle → CONFIRMED bad via claim 1's evidence (the worst angular case in Huang's data).
- BUT bundles of parallel edges crossing another bundle in one clean block: the block-crossing literature (Fink/Pupyrev/Wolff, Block Crossings in Storyline Visualizations; The Bundled Crossing Number, arXiv:1608.08161 / s2-10-1007-978-3-319-50106-2-31; Bundled Crossings Revisited, arXiv:1812.04263) argues the OPPOSITE of the owner's intuition: grouping many pairwise crossings into one grid-like block crossing reduces visual complexity, invoking Gestalt good continuation — though these authors explicitly note NO user study has validated block-crossing readability. So the literature would reorganize a parallel-bundle crossing into one clean, large-angle block, not scatter it.
- Edge-bundling side: merging parallel edges into shared curves is empirically double-edged — Towards Unambiguous Edge Bundling (Bach et al., doi-10-1109-tvcg-2016-2598958) and Edge-Path Bundling (doi-10-1109-tvcg-2021-3114795): bundling creates connectivity AMBIGUITY and hurts path tracing ("very difficult to keep track of which edge goes to which endpoint"), while helping macro-structure tasks; Nguyen/Eades/Hong faithfulness work (doi-10-1109-pacificvis-2013-6596147) shows bundling is inherently information-unfaithful.
- Design implication: penalize small-angle crossings between near-parallel edges (already covered by the angle metric); do NOT add a penalty for co-located parallel crossings per se — if a bundle must cross a bundle, make it cross once, together, at a large angle (a block crossing), and keep individual edges separable (our polylines stay separate, avoiding the bundling-ambiguity failure mode).

CLAIM 4 — "Edges that turn backwards against the LR flow look bad": CONFIRMED (strong mechanism, thin direct manipulation).

- Huang, Eades, Hong, "A graph reading behavior: Geodesic-path tendency" (PacificVis 2009, doi-10-1109-pacificvis-2009-4906848) + "How People Read Graphs" (2005): in path tasks, eyes preferentially follow links heading geometrically TOWARD the target; edges/branches leading away from the geodesic direction trigger reorientation, longer fixations, and errors; drawings with strong geodesic-path tendency measurably improve performance. A backward-turning edge in an LR layout is precisely an anti-geodesic segment.
- The monotone-drawing program (Angelini et al., Monotone Drawings of Graphs, jgaa-2667) is explicitly motivated by this evidence: paths that are monotone in the reading direction are easier to follow. Layered/Sugiyama convention research (consistent flow direction as aesthetic C9) and Purchase's UML studies treat consistent flow direction as a user-preferred convention.
- Nuance: no study directly A/B-tests backward-turning polyline segments; the evidence is convergent-indirect. Also the eye-tracking paper shows the penalty is concentrated in path-following tasks — for pure overview/structure tasks backward turns matter less.
- Design implication: make X-monotonicity (no dx<0 segments) a per-edge constraint in the curve refinement — the step/curve transplants can introduce back-turns at stubs; this is cheap to guard and the literature says it is the single most defensible of the owner's pattern claims for a directed LR diagram.

CLAIM 5 — "Edges crossing into their entry/exit nodes / over source-target cards look bad": CONFIRMED.

- Edge-node overlap is a canonical demerit: Purchase, "Metrics for Graph Drawing Aesthetics" (JVLC 2002, openalex-10-1006-jvlc-2002-0232) formalizes node-edge overlap as a measured aesthetic violation; recent viewpoint-preference work (Show Me Your Best Side, arXiv:2506.09212) finds Edge-Node Overlap among the top indicators of user preference, and articulates the mechanism: an edge passing over a node can falsely imply a connection that does not exist, and occlusion obscures node content — for our diagram, an edge over a resource CARD occludes its label/icon, the worst case of this.
- Endpoint-region crossings specifically: Huang's eye-tracking paper (arXiv:0810.4431) — node-task errors (6.25% and 25%) were attributed to poor ANGULAR RESOLUTION where edges attach: "when the angular resolution is poor, the task will be harder, thus causing more errors in counting the number of edges that a node has"; subjects wanted edges cleanly distributed around the node. A foreign edge crossing through the endpoint fan destroys exactly this attachment legibility. Ware et al., "Cognitive Measurements of Graph Aesthetics" (2002, doi-10-1057-palgrave-ivs-9500013) adds that path continuity at each node (angular deviation of the continuing edge) is a strong predictor of response time — clutter in the endpoint region breaks the continuation cue where it matters most.
- Nuance: I found no study isolating "crossing an edge NEAR the endpoint vs mid-span", but the composed evidence (occlusion demerit + angular-resolution errors + continuity at nodes) makes an endpoint-exclusion-zone penalty well-grounded.
- Design implication: our perpendicular exit stubs are exactly right (they maximize attachment legibility and continuity); add (a) a hard no-overlap constraint between an edge's polyline and ANY card including its own source/target beyond the stub, and (b) a weighted penalty for crossings occurring within an endpoint radius (e.g., 1 stub-length) of either card.

FACTORS THE OWNER DID NOT NAME (ranked high by the literature — do not over-optimize the named five):

1. Crossing COUNT on the traced path — Purchase 1997 ("Which aesthetic has the greatest effect on human understanding?") found edge-crossing minimization the single most influential aesthetic, ahead of bends and symmetry (Validating Graph Drawing Aesthetics, s2-10-1007-bfb0021827, cited 195x in-corpus). Refinement rule: any curve tweak that raises the crossings count (currently 92, already the best arm) is likely a net loss regardless of angle gains. Importantly it is PATH-crossings, not total crossings, that predict performance (Ware et al. 2002).
2. Continuity / good continuation at nodes and crossings — Ware et al. 2002 found average continuation-angle deviation a strong RT predictor. Our curve arm's endpointRes of 51.5° (vs 14.1° for step, 23.3° baseline) is its worst literature-facing number: consider blending the perpendicular stub into the curve so the tangent at the card is perpendicular but the overall continuation deviation drops.
3. Edge length — Huang's subjects: "long edges need more time to reconfirm"; long shortest-paths were missed. Corridor detours that lengthen edges have a measured cost.
4. Bend count matters LESS than expected — Purchase's experiments repeatedly found bend minimization weaker than crossing minimization; the step arm's 252 bends are not automatically fatal, but its +64% crossings (176) are.
5. Curvature itself is a risk — Xu, Rooney, Passmore et al., "A User Study on Curved Edges in Graph Visualization" (TVCG 2012): straight edges outperformed curved; performance degraded with curvature; curved links were the worst representation in a related directed-edge study (Holten & van Wijk direction study). The owner's "curve looks best" verdict is a preference judgment; the performance literature says keep curvature LOW (near-straight beziers), which also serves the nearFlat=0.09 win the curve arm already has. Domain-Specific Rules Override Aesthetic Graph Drawing Criteria (s2-10-1007-978-3-031-71291-3-4) is a useful frame: for infrastructure diagrams, domain conventions (containment hulls, LR rank flow) legitimately outrank generic aesthetics — consistent with trusting the owner's eyeball as tie-breaker but validating each pattern penalty against task-performance evidence, as done here.
